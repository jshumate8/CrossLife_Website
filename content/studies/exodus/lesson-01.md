# Introduction to Exodus

## Overview

Exodus is the second book of the Bible and continues the narrative begun in Genesis. The name "Exodus" comes from the Greek word meaning "exit" or "departure," referring to Israel's dramatic deliverance from Egyptian slavery. In Hebrew, the book is called "Shemot" (שְׁמוֹת), meaning "Names," from its opening words: "These are the names of..."

Exodus tells the foundational story of Israel's national birth—their redemption from bondage, the giving of the Law at Mount Sinai, and the establishment of the Tabernacle as God's dwelling place among His people.

## Author and Date

### Traditional View
- **Author**: Moses
- **Date of Writing**: Approximately 1446-1406 BC (during the wilderness wanderings)
- **Time Period Covered**: Approximately 1526-1446 BC (from Moses' birth to the completion of the Tabernacle)

Moses was uniquely qualified to write Exodus:
- He was educated in Egyptian wisdom and culture (Acts 7:22)
- He personally experienced and led all the events described
- He received direct revelation from God at Mount Sinai
- The New Testament consistently attributes the book to Moses (Mark 12:26; Luke 2:22-23; John 1:45)

## Structure

Exodus can be divided into three major sections:

### Part 1: Deliverance from Egypt (Chapters 1-18)
- Oppression in Egypt (1-2)
- The Call of Moses (3-6)
- The Ten Plagues (7-12)
- The Exodus and Red Sea Crossing (13-15)
- Journey to Sinai (16-18)

### Part 2: Revelation at Sinai (Chapters 19-24)
- Preparation to meet God (19)
- The Ten Commandments (20)
- The Book of the Covenant (21-23)
- Covenant Ratification (24)

### Part 3: Instructions for the Tabernacle (Chapters 25-40)
- Plans for the Tabernacle and Priesthood (25-31)
- The Golden Calf and Covenant Renewal (32-34)
- Building the Tabernacle (35-40)

## Major Themes

### 1. Redemption
The central theme of Exodus is God's redemption of His people. The Passover and the Red Sea crossing become the defining moments of Israel's history and powerful symbols of salvation.

### 2. The Covenant
God establishes a covenant relationship with Israel at Sinai, making them His treasured possession and holy nation.

### 3. The Presence of God
From the burning bush to the pillar of cloud and fire to the Tabernacle, God's presence with His people is emphasized throughout.

### 4. The Character of God
Exodus reveals God as:
- Faithful to His promises (remembering the covenant with Abraham)
- Powerful and sovereign (defeating Egypt's gods)
- Holy and righteous (giving the Law)
- Merciful and gracious (forgiving after the golden calf)

### 5. The Law and Holiness
The giving of the Law establishes standards for living in covenant relationship with a holy God.

### 6. Worship
Detailed instructions for the Tabernacle show that worship must be on God's terms, centered on sacrifice and mediation.

## Purpose

Exodus was written to:

1. **Record Israel's National Birth**: To document how God formed a covenant nation from enslaved people.

2. **Establish the Covenant Relationship**: To define the terms of God's relationship with Israel through the Law.

3. **Reveal God's Character**: To show God's power, faithfulness, holiness, and desire to dwell with His people.

4. **Provide a Pattern of Redemption**: The Exodus becomes the paradigm for understanding salvation throughout Scripture.

5. **Instruct in Worship**: The Tabernacle plans teach that approach to God requires mediator, sacrifice, and obedience.

## Key Theological Concepts

### The Passover
The Passover lamb, whose blood protects from judgment, points forward to Christ, "our Passover lamb" (1 Corinthians 5:7).

### The Law
The Law reveals God's character and standards, shows humanity's sin, and points to the need for a Savior (Romans 3:20; Galatians 3:24).

### The Tabernacle
Every element of the Tabernacle points to Christ:
- The way to God (the one entrance)
- Sacrifice for sin (the altar)
- Cleansing (the bronze basin)
- God's presence (the Most Holy Place)
- The bread of life, light of the world, intercessor (the furniture)

## Relevance for Today

Exodus speaks powerfully to contemporary believers:

- **Liberation**: God delivers from all forms of bondage—sin, addiction, oppression
- **Identity**: We find our identity in God's redemptive work, not our circumstances
- **Obedience**: God's commands are expressions of His love and wisdom
- **God's Presence**: Like Israel, we are called to be a people where God dwells (1 Corinthians 3:16)
- **Worship**: True worship approaches God on His terms, through the one mediator, Christ

## Key Verses

- **Exodus 3:14** - "God said to Moses, 'I AM WHO I AM.'"
- **Exodus 6:6-7** - "I will bring you out from under the burdens of the Egyptians... I will take you to be my people, and I will be your God."
- **Exodus 12:13** - "The blood shall be a sign for you... when I see the blood, I will pass over you."
- **Exodus 19:5-6** - "You shall be my treasured possession among all peoples... a kingdom of priests and a holy nation."
- **Exodus 20:2-3** - "I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt... You shall have no other gods before me."
- **Exodus 33:14** - "My presence will go with you, and I will give you rest."
- **Exodus 34:6-7** - The LORD's self-revelation of His compassionate and gracious character

## Connection to the New Testament

Exodus is foundational to New Testament theology:

- **Jesus as the New Moses**: Matthew presents Jesus as a new and greater Moses (Matthew 2-7)
- **The Passover and the Cross**: Jesus' death occurs at Passover; He is our Passover Lamb (John 1:29; 1 Corinthians 5:7)
- **The Law Fulfilled**: Jesus fulfills the Law (Matthew 5:17; Romans 10:4)
- **The New Covenant**: Jesus establishes a new and better covenant (Luke 22:20; Hebrews 8-10)
- **The True Tabernacle**: Jesus is the true temple where God dwells with humanity (John 1:14; 2:19-21)
- **Freedom in Christ**: The Exodus pattern of redemption is applied to salvation in Christ (Galatians 5:1)

## How to Study Exodus

As we work through Exodus, we will:

1. Follow the historical narrative chronologically
2. Examine key Hebrew words and concepts
3. Understand the cultural and historical context
4. Explore how each section reveals God's character
5. Trace typological connections to Christ
6. Apply timeless principles to contemporary life

## The Gospel in Exodus

Exodus is fundamentally a gospel book, illustrating salvation:

1. **Slavery**: Like Israel in Egypt, we are enslaved to sin (Romans 6:17)
2. **God's Initiative**: God heard their groaning; salvation is His work (Ephesians 2:8-9)
3. **A Redeemer**: God sent Moses; God sent Jesus (Galatians 4:4-5)
4. **Blood Sacrifice**: The Passover lamb's blood saves; Christ's blood redeems (1 Peter 1:18-19)
5. **Freedom**: The Red Sea baptism into new life; baptism identifies with Christ (1 Corinthians 10:1-2)
6. **Journey**: The wilderness journey to the Promised Land; our pilgrimage to heaven (Hebrews 11:13-16)
7. **God's Presence**: The Tabernacle among them; Christ and the Spirit dwelling in us (John 1:14; Ephesians 2:22)
